The math behind the chart

Is "no limping" right? What the math says, seat by seat

1
The verdict: the TikTok is right, with two exceptions.

Solvers are programs that calculate the strategy nobody can beat. At normal stack sizes (100 big blinds), they never open-limp from UTG to the Button: they raise or fold. Limping is only used in two spots:

• Small blind, when everyone folded to you: solvers mix raises and limps against the big blind. Most simple charts (like the one above) use raise-or-fold here, which is also fine.
• Over-limping: when someone already limped, calling behind with small pairs or suited hands can be right (quiz hand 8).

2
Why later seats raise more: fewer players can wake up with a big hand.

Every player behind you is a chance that someone holds a monster. The chance grows fast with more players:

"Premium" = QQ, KK, AA or AK (2.6% of deals each player). "Good hand" = any top-15% hand. From UTG, 3 times out of 4 someone behind has a good hand. From the Button, only 1 time in 4.

3
Why the Button can raise 45% of hands: the steal math.

On the Button you raise to win the blinds (1.5 big blinds already in the pot). A raise to 2.5 BB risks 2.5 to win 1.5. It profits right away if the blinds fold more than 2.5 ÷ (2.5 + 1.5) = 62.5% of the time, even with 7-2. And when you do get called, you act last for the rest of the hand, which is worth extra.

4
Why the Low stakes chart is tighter.

The solver assumes opponents who fold and call correctly. At $1/$2 live, people call raises far too often, and a live raise is bigger (4–5 BB). A 4 BB steal needs the blinds to fold 4 ÷ 5.5 = 73%, which rarely happens. So you'll usually be called, often by 2 players. That's when hand strength matters more than the steal, and the tighter chart wins more. The casino's rake (its cut of each pot) also punishes weak hands in small pots.

Rule of thumb: tough online table → Solver chart. Loose live table where many people call → Low stakes chart.

5
The big blind: pot odds say defend a lot.

The Button raises to 2.5 BB and the small blind folds. The pot is 2.5 + 0.5 + your 1 = 4 BB, and you call 1.5 more. You need to win 1.5 ÷ 5.5 = 27%. Most hands have that against a wide Button range. That's why solvers defend the big blind with many hands (30–60% depending on who raised, most against the Button). Against an early-seat raise, defend much less: their hands are stronger.

In large hand databases, the Button is the most profitable seat and the blinds are the only seats that lose money over time. Position is money: you act last, you see what everyone did, and you control the pot.

Should you memorize the "winning odds" chart?

Keep it as a reference (it's the Win % · 1 opp view above), but don't memorize it. Memorize the raise chart instead. Three reasons:

1. It assumes one opponent with a random hand. Real players who call have real hands, and low-stakes pots are often 3–5 players. Tap 2, 3 and 4 opps and every number shrinks: AA falls from 85% to 56%.

2. It ignores how a hand plays after the flop. K2o shows 51% and 76s only 45%, so K2o looks better. It isn't. With 4 players 76s wins more (21% vs 17%), and when K2o hits a king you have the worst kicker and lose big pots. 76s makes straights and flushes that win big pots.

3. It ignores your seat. The same hand is a raise on the Button and a fold under the gun. The chart can't tell you that.

The only parts worth remembering: pairs win 50–85% heads-up, a hand with an ace about 55–65%, and even the worst hands (3-2, 7-2) still win about 1 time in 3. Preflop edges are small, so decisions after the flop are where you actually win money.

Call or fold his bet?

Bluff or not? Should I call his river bet?

This is NOT about your cards' odds.

On the river no more cards are coming. Your hand is already finished: it's either better than his or it isn't. So the only question left is: how often does he have a worse hand or a bluff? The math below tells you how often that must be for calling to make money.

1. Pick his bet size (the example pot is €10):

Why do we say "out of 10"?

Because you can never know what he has this one time. But you will face this same kind of bet again and again. A good call is one that makes money if you make it every time this spot comes up.

A coin example first. A friend says: "Flip a coin. Heads, I pay you €30. Tails, you pay me €20." Would you play? Yes, of course. You might lose this one flip, but over 10 flips you win about 5 × €30 = €150 and lose 5 × €20 = €100. You're up €50. One flip can go wrong; the deal is still good.

Poker is the same, except it isn't 50/50. The only unknown is how often he's bluffing. Here's a real hand:

The basics

Fold, call or raise?

Your buttons before the flop

Before any cards are dealt, the two blinds are forced to bet. So when it's your turn there is already a bet on the table (the big blind). That's why "check" doesn't exist for you yet:

FOLDThrow your cards away. Costs €0.
CALL = LIMPPay just the big blind (e.g. €2 at €1/€2). This is "limping". It's what feels like "checking", but you are paying.
RAISEMake the bet bigger (e.g. to €8–10). You take control of the hand.
CHECKOnly possible in the big blind when nobody raised (you already paid), and after the flop when nobody has bet yet.

Why fold an "ok" hand instead of limping? An ok hand like K-9, Q-8 or A-6 offsuit is usually dominated: when it hits, someone with K-Q, Q-J or A-K hit too, with a better second card. Then you lose a big pot with "top pair". Limping also invites everyone in (the Equity lab shows K9o dropping from 58% to 24% against 4 players). The €2 is cheap; the pot you lose later is not. So: good enough to play → raise. Not good enough to raise → fold, and save your money for better spots.

Remember it like this

Break-even line = 100% ÷ players at the pot. 2 players → 50%. 3 → 33%. 4 → 25%. 5 → 20%.

Win more often than the line → you make money over time (you're the favourite). Win less → you lose money, even though you still win some pots (you're the underdog).

Surprise: in a 5-player pot, pocket tens lose 64 times out of 100 and still make money, because they only needed to win 20.

Simplified: everyone puts the same money in and every hand is played to the end. Real poker adds betting and folding after the flop, but the idea stays the same.

beyond limping

12 pro tips for low stakes

Low-stakes players make the same few mistakes: they call too much, bluff too little, and play too many hands. These tips are built to profit from exactly that.

The truth about "winning every game"

Nobody wins every hand or every session, not even the best players in the world. Cards run bad for weeks. What you can control is making better decisions than the table. Over a few thousand hands that edge turns into profit. Play with money you can afford to lose, set a stop-loss (for example 3 buy-ins), and walk away when you're tired or angry.
